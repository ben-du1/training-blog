My Personal Experience With Reciepts

I used to touch receipts. I am not proud of it. For years, I accepted receipts without questioning their origin, chemical composition, or purpose. I stuffed them into my pockets. I kept them in my wallet. Sometimes, I would even hold a receipt in one hand while eating a sandwich with the other. I was young. I was ignorant. I trusted the printer. Everything changed when I discovered BPA. I began researching thermal paper and realized that the seemingly innocent slips of paper I had been accumulating were potentially exposing me to endocrine-disrupting chemicals. Every trip to Target. Every unnecessary purchase at Walmart. Every time I ordered a hamburger. The evidence had been sitting in my glove compartment the entire time. Naturally, I began asking questions. Why are receipts so smooth? Why does scratching them sometimes leave a dark mark? Why does the cashier insist on handing me a physical record of a transaction that my bank has already digitally documented? The deeper I investigated, the more disturbing things became. I discovered a scientific study demonstrating that certain hand sanitizers could dramatically increase the transfer of BPA from thermal paper onto human skin. I HAD BEEN SANITIZING MY HANDS BEFORE TOUCHING RECEIPTS. I thought I was protecting myself. Under certain conditions, I may have been increasing my chemical exposure. The irony was unbearable. I immediately began questioning my entire relationship with paper. I emptied my wallet. I investigated my grocery store's receipt policy. I started requesting digital receipts. Whenever a cashier asked whether I wanted a receipt, I declined with the conviction of a man who had uncovered a decades-long industrial oversight. My friends told me I was overreacting. But had my friends read the 2014 PLOS ONE study on BPA absorption? Had they investigated the endocrine-disrupting properties of BPS? Did they even understand the difference between conventional and thermal printing? Of course not. To this day, I cannot prove that touching receipts has caused me any personal health problems. Scientifically, that would require evidence I do not possess. But I can tell you this: I no longer willingly participate in unnecessary bisphenol exposure. Whenever I see someone casually stuffing a receipt into their pocket, I see my former self. Innocent. Uninformed. Chemically vulnerable. Sometimes, I wonder how different my life would have been if someone had simply told me about thermal paper. I cannot change my past. But I can refuse my receipt. And so can you.
